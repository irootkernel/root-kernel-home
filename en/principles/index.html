Principles

The nine principles decide what Root Kernel builds, postpones, and refuses. Every product, technology, and tool is judged by whether its behavior matches them.

  1. We build trust through structure, not effort alone.

  2. We define the specification before writing code. Without a specification, implementation does not begin.

  3. We make the same input produce the same result. A result that cannot be reproduced is not evidence.

  4. We do not let unverified work pass. When the verdict is unclear, we stop.

  5. We give AI broad autonomy while keeping human decision rights explicit.

  6. People set intent and boundaries. AI executes within them, and each reviews the other's work. Approval belongs to people.

  7. We do not lose a user's work or pass unfinished work off as success.

  8. We leave records in a form that can support the next decision.

  9. We do not choose between products and foundational technology. Products expose problems; the technology rebuilds the products around what we learn.

Why trust needs structure

A process that depends only on careful people and meticulous review breaks as volume grows. Once AI produces more changes than people can inspect, individual effort can no longer sustain trust.

Root Kernel defines boundaries in specifications, keeps reproducible records as evidence, and blocks unvalidated states from advancing. Identifying the accepted revision lets the team recover from failure using the same approved baseline.

Small teams need this more

Large organizations can add reviewers; small teams cannot. Specifications narrow the review surface, deterministic validation removes errors that do not need human judgment, and approval gates expose the decisions that do.

Root Kernel applies this discipline to its own development first. We do not recommend a practice we do not use ourselves.

What we refuse to do

  • We do not present unfinished work as complete because some features are ready.
  • We do not substitute hidden interface elements for authorization. The server enforces permission boundaries directly.
  • We do not trap user data inside an interface. We keep it in a portable form.
  • We do not give tools approval authority. A tool must state the limits of its authority first.
  • We do not treat irreproducible results as evidence. A passing result must be reproducible.
  • We do not bypass an approval gate because work is urgent. If the gate must change, we change the gate itself.
  • We do not count work that cannot use our structure as part of the company's direction.
